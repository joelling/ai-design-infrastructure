# The Designers Who Decide Best
### Agentic AI and what changes — and what doesn't — in the practice of design

## 00 — Cover

There is a story being told about AI and design. It goes like this: AI will make designers faster, teams smaller, and output larger. The productivity case is so obvious it barely needs making. The workflow transforms. The profession democratises. Everyone wins.

This talk is about why that story is incomplete — and what the more honest, more interesting, and ultimately more demanding story looks like.

> Subtitle: *"On agentic AI and what changes — and what doesn't — in the practice of design"*

## 01 — The Story We're Telling

Design has always drifted toward its artefacts. The Figma file needed to be consistent before the brief needed to be honest. The delivery date was legible in a way the quality of thinking behind it was not. Every design field has found its own version of this drift — industrial design toward the precision of the object, architecture toward the elegance of the form, digital product toward the fidelity of the interface.

The correction, across a century of practice, has always pointed to the same place: back to the person. Back to the purpose. Back to the intent before the artefact.

This is a talk about a moment when that correction becomes structurally possible for the first time — and simultaneously harder to make than it has ever been.

## 02 — The Reversal

Agentic AI is entering the design process, and the immediate assumption is straightforward: designers will work faster. More output, less time, smaller teams. The productivity case writes itself.

It is a compelling promise. It is also, in most cases, wrong.

Not because AI is incapable. But because the premise misunderstands where the cost of design actually lives.

> **Slide 02 — Big statement**
> *"Agentic AI is entering the design process. The assumption is straightforward: designers will work faster."*

# Act One — The Judgment Scale

---

## 03 — The Fallacy

Here is the problem with the productivity argument. If an AI generates a persona and a designer must review it — correct it, reshape it to reflect what they actually know about the user — then the output generation cost has simply been replaced by a review and iteration cost. The work has not been removed. It has been redistributed.

For judgment-heavy outputs — personas, journey maps, visual direction, story backbones — the AI's first pass is often plausible but generic. It has the right shape without the right substance. The designer still has to do the interpretive work. They are just doing it on top of someone else's draft, which is not always faster and sometimes slower.

If the measure of success is "time to produce an artefact," agentic AI disappoints more often than it delivers. That is the wrong measure.

> **Slide 03 — Big statement (reversal)**
> *"It is a compelling promise. It is also, in most cases, wrong."*

---

## 04 — The Fallacy Explained

The fallacy is this: treating all design outputs as equivalent in cognitive cost, and assuming that if a machine generates them faster, the cost has been removed.

But design outputs are not equivalent. At one end of a spectrum: work that requires deep interpretation, empathy, judgment — the kind that emerges from a researcher who has sat in the field, felt the tension in the data, and made a call about what matters. At the other end: work that is high-volume, rule-based, and verifiable as correct or incorrect against a known standard.

These are not points on the same continuum. They are different kinds of work. And the first kind cannot be accelerated by delegation to a machine.

> **Slide 04 — Content + text (The Fallacy)**
> Headline: *"The Fallacy"*
> Body: *"If an AI generates a persona and a designer must review it, the output generation cost has been replaced by a review and iteration cost. The work hasn't been removed. It has been redistributed."*

---

## 05 — The Scale

The judgment scale runs from left to right.

On the left: personas, journey maps, visual language, story backbones, problem reframes. Work that requires a human who has done the research, absorbed the tension, and made a call. No prompt structure changes this. The insight must come from somewhere real before it can be encoded into a system.

On the right: state inventories, accessibility pattern application, token system construction, traceability validation, component binding, documentation population. High-volume, rule-based, verifiable. A designer reviewing this work is checking for correctness. They are not asking whether it feels right.

The productivity gains from agentic AI are real. They are concentrated on the right side of that scale — in the execution phases of design. The further left you go — into discovery, into user models, into the structural decisions about what to build — the more the designer is still doing the thinking and the agent is handling the formatting.

> Right label: *"RULE-BASED"* — State inventories / Accessibility patterns / Token binding / BRD tagging

## 06 — The Implication

The implication of the judgment scale is significant. And it runs counter to the instinct of most organisations facing pressure to move faster.

You cannot staff for a smaller team by removing discovery and definition capacity. You can staff for a smaller team by removing execution capacity — because that is what is actually being automated.

An organisation that cuts research and user model investment in response to AI tooling has not reduced the cost of design. It has removed the part of the process that determines whether the design is worth building. The pipeline will execute faithfully from whatever foundations it is given. Thin foundations, executed at full fidelity, produce consistent noise.

> **Slide 06 — Big statement**
> *"You cannot staff for a smaller team by removing discovery capacity. You can remove execution capacity. That is what is actually being automated."*

# Act Two — What Execution Actually Did

---

## 07 — The Old Correction Mechanism

In the old way — the pre-pipeline way — execution was slow. A designer who spent six weeks building screens was six weeks inside the decisions. Close enough to notice when something didn't hold. Close enough to catch the implication of a choice made in week two when it became apparent in week five.

This proximity was accidental. Nobody designed it. Execution consumed the designer. But that consumption was also a form of thinking. A thin persona got quietly corrected mid-wireframe when the designer noticed the model didn't fit. A missed interaction state surfaced in Figma and got specified under pressure. A weak information architecture got reworked over two days nobody planned for.

The cost of weak foundations was real — but it was diffuse, absorbed by the weeks of iteration that filled the middle of every project. Execution was expensive. It was also the correction mechanism. A designer who was sharp in execution could compensate for a beginning that was thinner than it should have been.

> **Slide 07 — Content + text**
> Headline: *"The Old Correction Mechanism"*
> Body: *"In the old way, execution was slow — and that slowness kept the designer close to the decisions. Proximity to the artefact was also proximity to the problem. Weak foundations could be corrected through iteration. That mechanism is gone."*

---

## 08 — The Pipeline Removes That

The new pipeline changes this completely.

A pipeline that enforces decisions consistently, cascades them automatically, and traces them bidirectionally does not forgive thin foundations. It amplifies them. A persona built from one stakeholder assumption and marked with an optimistic evidence tier passes every downstream check. The traceability validator confirms the link. The audit finds no violations. The canvas briefs are comprehensive. Everything is correct.

And the product ships as a well-structured, internally consistent, fully traced version of the wrong thing.

The cost of error no longer distributes across the middle of the project. It concentrates at the end — where it is expensive, late, and visible. The only way to avoid it is to not have made the error in the first place. The front of the process now carries more weight than it ever did.

> **Slide 08 — Big statement**
> *"The pipeline did not reduce the cost of being wrong. It relocated it."*

---

## 09 — Coherent Wrongness

The old failure mode was inconsistency: artefacts that didn't match each other, states that weren't specified, design decisions that lived only in the designer's head. These failures were visible. They showed up as gaps — missing components, mismatched labels, engineering questions no handoff document answered.

The new failure mode is coherent wrongness.

A pipeline executing from weak foundations produces outputs that pass every structural check. The traceability links are present. The evidence tiers are populated. The component variants exist. The audit finds nothing to flag. The artefact is complete. And it is wrong — not in any way the pipeline can detect, because the pipeline evaluates structure, not truth.

This failure mode is harder to catch than a gap. A gap is visible. Coherent wrongness looks finished.

> **Slide 09 — Two-column contrast**
> Left headline: *"Old failure mode"*
> Left body: *"Inconsistency. Gaps. Missing states. Visible in review."*
> Right headline: *"New failure mode"*
> Right body: *"Coherent wrongness. Structurally complete. Audit-passing. Invisible until the product meets the world."*

---

## 10 — The Mirror, Not the Mind

The pipeline produces four things that did not previously exist in a reliable form.

An evidence architecture: every claim about users travels with its confidence level. A constraint system: nothing enters scope without tracing to a user need. Computable intent: a design rule set once is enforced by the pipeline across every component, every variant, every artefact downstream. A translation chain: every step from intent to shipped screen is explicit and traceable.

Each of these is only as good as what it was built from. The evidence architecture enforces whatever evidence tier the designer assigned. The constraint system enforces whatever stories the designer committed. The computable intent propagates whatever rules the designer encoded.

The pipeline is a high-fidelity mirror of the designer's foundations. It does not interpret. It does not compensate. It reflects.

> **Slide 10 — Big statement**
> *"The pipeline is a high-fidelity mirror of the designer's foundations. It does not interpret. It does not compensate. It reflects."*

# Act Three — One Layer Upstream

---

## 11 — Two Acts That Share a Name

Here is where the story gets more complicated. Because the failure mode described in the last section does not begin in the design pipeline. It begins one layer upstream — in research.

User research is usually described as a service. It serves delivery. It is timed to project phases, categorised by method, and evaluated by whether it produced usable outputs in time. But what we call "research" contains two fundamentally different acts that happen to share a name.

The first is contact: being present with a human in a specific moment, attending to what they say and what they don't, constructing an understanding of their world from a position inside it. The second is synthesis: processing signals — transcripts, tickets, surveys, usage data — into patterns.

Both are valuable. They are not substitutes. Contact generates novel evidence. Synthesis reorganises evidence that already exists. Treating them as points on the same continuum is the framing error that AI now makes consequential.

> **Slide 11 — Two-column contrast**
> Headline: *"Two Acts That Share a Name"*
> Left headline: *"Contact"*
> Left body: *"Being present with a human. Attending to what they say and don't say. Generating novel evidence from a world the product hasn't yet modelled."*
> Right headline: *"Synthesis"*
> Right body: *"Processing signals into patterns. Reorganising evidence that already exists. Producing insight from a corpus — but only from the corpus."*

---

## 12 — What AI Actually Changes in Research

AI is now separating these two acts by economics.

Synthesis is becoming cheap, fast, and largely automatable. Transcription, thematic coding, affinity clustering, persona drafting from raw notes — work that once consumed weeks now takes hours. Passive signal mining — the large-scale analysis of support tickets, app reviews, forum posts — surfaces frequency distributions of pain signals, terminology, and workarounds across populations far larger than any study could recruit.

What none of this changes is the epistemology of contact.

AI can synthesise any corpus. It cannot extend the corpus. The researcher who is physically present with a person — watching them hesitate before a term they would never use in a survey, noticing the workaround built into a workflow that no ticket ever described, hearing the offhand comment after the formal interview ends that reframes everything that came before it — is doing something categorically different from the researcher reading a synthesised report of existing signals.

Contact generates the finding that was not already in the data. Synthesis cannot.

> **Slide 12 — Content + text**
> Headline: *"What AI Changes in Research"*
> Body: *"AI can synthesise any corpus. It cannot extend the corpus. The unexpected finding — the thing the study was not designed to surface but surfaced anyway — is precisely what a synthesised corpus cannot generate."*

---

## 13 — The Bottleneck Relocates

The organisation that has replaced field visits with passive signal mining has not done less research. It has done different research with different epistemological reach — and it may not have noticed the difference, because the output looks the same. A themes report extracted from four hundred support tickets has the format of research. It will be referenced in the design brief with equal weight to a field study. And it may be missing the one thing a day in the field would have surfaced: the unexpected finding that reframes the problem, the behaviour no ticket described because users had normalised it, the need so fundamental that no one thought to complain about its absence.

This is the coherent wrongness risk applied one layer upstream. The pipeline executes faithfully from whatever the designer's foundations contain. What the foundations contain is shaped by what the research provided. If the researcher believes that a synthesised corpus is the same kind of evidence as witnessed contact, the error is invisible in the pipeline — right up until the product meets the world it was not built for.

When synthesis is expensive, the research bottleneck is back-end: the analyst's limiting factor is the time cost of processing. When synthesis is cheap, the bottleneck moves to front-end: the limiting factor is inquiry design — the skill of deciding what is worth knowing, identifying who holds the knowledge, and creating the conditions in which they reveal it.

The burden relocates to the front. Less visible. Less legible. More consequential.

> **Slide 13 — Big statement**
> *"The burden relocates to the front, where it is less visible and less legible — but more consequential."*

# Act Four — What the Value Actually Is

---

## 14 — A Better Question

So let us reframe. Not: how fast can the AI generate? But: what does the designer no longer have to hold in their head?

Design work carries two kinds of cognitive load. The first is the thinking — the interpretation of research, the judgment calls, the empathy, the synthesis. The second is the tracking — which artefact version is current, whether a copy change propagated through three tools, whether the accessibility constraints from last month made it into the component spec.

The second kind is not design thinking. It is overhead. And it scales with project complexity in a way that eventually overwhelms the first kind.

The value of agentic AI is not that it replaces design thinking. It is that it absorbs the overhead — which gives the thinking room to breathe.

> Left headline: *"The Thinking"*
> Left body: *"Interpretation, empathy, synthesis, judgment — what users need, what matters, what to build"*
> Right headline: *"The Tracking"*
> Right body: *"Which artefact version is current. Whether a copy change propagated. Whether last month's constraints made it into the spec."*

## 15 — Faster Validation Cycles

When the time from insight to testable artefact is shorter, the design can validate earlier and more often. Not before Figma — before the first wireframe. Not once per sprint — after every structural change.

Agentic AI does not make design faster so much as it makes iteration cheaper. The round you could not previously afford — the one where you return to the concept after user testing rather than shipping the third draft — becomes viable. The design converges on what users actually need rather than what the team assumed at the start.

The productivity gain is not in the first pass. It is in the rounds that were previously too expensive to run.

> **Slide 15 — Content + text**
> Headline: *"Faster Validation Cycles"*
> Body: *"Agentic AI doesn't make design faster so much as it makes iteration cheaper. The round you couldn't previously afford becomes viable. The design converges on what users need, not what the team assumed."*

---

## 16 — Design Intent Survives Handoff

In a traditional delivery pipeline, the "why" behind a design decision degrades as it moves downstream. The nuance that lived in the designer's head gets lost in translation to a developer ticket. The user insight that shaped a decision gets optimised away in a sprint because no one remembered where it came from.

Traceability enforced by the system — every decision connected to a persona, a story, or a principle, bidirectionally validated — means the reasoning travels with the output. The brief doesn't just say what to build. It carries the full chain of reasoning that explains why.

Design intent becomes durable in a way it has never been before.

> **Slide 16 — Content + text**
> Headline: *"Design Intent Survives Handoff"*
> Body: *"Traceability enforced by the system means the reasoning travels with the output. The 'why' behind a decision no longer degrades as it moves downstream."*

---

## 17 — Standards Hold Under Pressure

Quality in human-only teams is aspirational. Accessibility reviews, token discipline, traceability checks — these are the first things sacrificed when a deadline moves. They are the parts of the process that require time nobody has and produce outputs nobody can see.

An agentic process makes the quality floor structural rather than aspirational. The non-negotiables are enforced every run, regardless of how much pressure the team is under. Good practice becomes the path of least resistance rather than the exception.

The designer who previously spent energy policing consistency can now redirect that energy to the decisions that require judgment. The pipeline holds the line so the designer doesn't have to.

> **Slide 17 — Content + text**
> Headline: *"Standards Hold Under Pressure"*
> Body: *"Quality in human-only teams is aspirational. An agentic process makes the quality floor structural. The non-negotiables are enforced every run, regardless of deadline pressure."*

---

## 18 — User Understanding Accumulates

Research is usually locked in a researcher's head or a forgotten project file. When team members change or a project restarts after a gap, the understanding of users resets. Every new team inherits the brief but not the reasoning behind it.

A version-controlled artefact chain — personas with explicit evidence tiers, journey maps linked to discovery inputs, decisions traceable to the research that informed them — means understanding compounds rather than resets. New team members inherit the full design history. A decision made eighteen months ago and the context that motivated it are both available, versioned, traceable.

The system gets more valuable the longer it runs.

# Act Five — What Design Actually Is

---

## 19 — Design Is Not the Artefact

Every design field has found its own version of drift toward its artefacts. Industrial design toward the precision of the object. Architecture toward the elegance of the form. Digital product toward the fidelity of the interface.

Herbert Simon defined design as the transformation of existing conditions into preferred ones. Victor Papanek argued that design divorced from human consequence is not design at all — it is decoration with intent removed. Charles and Ray Eames spoke of every element earning its place — not through aesthetic justification, but through honest service to the person it was made for.

These are not aesthetic positions. They are claims about what design is. And they converge on the same point: the artefact is not the design. The design is the intent, the judgment, the decision about what preferred conditions look like and why. The artefact is the delivery of that decision.

The pipeline makes this distinction not just philosophical but operational. The pipeline handles the artefact. The designer handles the design.

> **Slide 19 — Big statement**
> *"The artefact is not the design. The design is the intent, the judgment, the decision about what preferred conditions look like and why."*

---

## 20 — The Correction Always Points the Same Way

Across a century of design practice, the correction to artefact drift has always pointed to the same place. Back to the person. Back to the purpose. Back to the intent before the artefact.

What changes with AI is not the direction of the correction. It is the structural conditions that make it possible — or that make it dangerously easy to skip.

The pipeline provides something no previous design practice could: genuine structural freedom to give attention to the people the product is for, before a single artefact is produced. The execution no longer demands the designer's constant presence. For the first time, the designer is free to be where the work has always needed them most — in the room with the user, before the brief is written.

But that freedom is not given. It is a choice. And it requires a discipline that the old execution-heavy model did not demand in the same way, because execution itself enforced proximity. Now the designer must choose proximity without the structure enforcing it.

> **Slide 20 — Content + text**
> Headline: *"The Correction Always Points the Same Way"*
> Body: *"For the first time, the pipeline provides structural freedom to attend to users before a single artefact is produced. That freedom is not given. It is a choice — and it requires a discipline the old model didn't demand."*

---

## 21 — Every Element Must Earn Its Place

Papanek's standard applies to the designer's decisions, not just the design's outputs. Every judgment call must earn its place. Every evidence claim must be honest about its confidence tier. Every walking skeleton must be challenged before it becomes the foundation for a cascade. Every problem reframe must happen before the brief is accepted, not after the screens are built.

This is a more demanding role than it might appear. It requires the designer to be present before there is anything to show — in the field, with the people the product is for, before the first mode has been invoked. It requires intellectual honesty at the moments that feel preliminary: acknowledging thin evidence rather than upgrading it, challenging the walking skeleton rather than accepting the first version, asking whether the problem is right before asking how to solve it.

Most of all, it requires the discipline to understand that the execution quality of the pipeline is now irrelevant to the outcome if the foundations were weak. A beautifully executed, fully traced, audit-passing product built from a hollow brief is not a design achievement. It is a precise, well-organised delivery of the wrong answer.

> **Slide 21 — Big statement**
> *"A beautifully executed, fully traced, audit-passing product built from a hollow brief is not a design achievement. It is a precise, well-organised delivery of the wrong answer."*

# Act Six — The Reframed Role

---

## 22 — Three Irreducible Functions

Creativity in design is largely remix — a recombination of known patterns, existing solutions, absorbed research, and design history. AI is very good at remix. But the judgment that governs the remix — what to combine, for whom, to what end, under what constraints — is not remix. It is the act that makes remix design rather than generation.

In a pipeline-supported design process, three functions remain irreducibly human.

The first is intent origination: deciding what preferred conditions look like for this user, in this context, with these constraints. This cannot be delegated. A system has no stake in whether the problem is worth solving.

The second is direction setting: making the judgment calls that shape the pipeline's execution — which stories form the walking skeleton, what evidence tier this persona warrants, how the interaction model should handle the edge case. These decisions determine what the pipeline will faithfully produce.

The third is drift detection: noticing when the pipeline's output has diverged from the intent that drove it — not because a structural check failed, but because something has shifted in the understanding of the problem that the pipeline cannot detect. This requires a designer who holds the original intent and reads against it, not one who reads for correctness.

None of these functions is replaceable. All of them require the designer's fullest attention. The pipeline does everything else.

> Centre: *"Direction setting"* — The judgment calls that shape the pipeline's execution. These decisions determine what the pipeline will faithfully produce.
> Right: *"Drift detection"* — Noticing when output has diverged from intent. Requires a designer who reads against intent, not just for correctness.

## 23 — A Shift in Where You Operate

The designer is not the person who manages execution. The pipeline does that.

The designer is not primarily the person who catches drift in review. The pipeline flags that. Review is necessary but it is not where the designer's highest value sits.

The designer is the person who gets the foundations right before the pipeline starts — and who understands, with clarity, that the pipeline will execute whatever those foundations contain, faithfully, at scale, without judgment about whether they were worth executing.

What moves away is the overhead: dependency tracking, artefact versioning, consistency enforcement, documentation population. What stays is everything that requires a human who has done the research, absorbed the tension in the data, and made a call about what matters.

The designer's leverage increases not because they work faster but because they work at a higher level of the stack.

> **Slide 23 — Two-column contrast**
> Headline: *"A Shift in Where You Operate"*
> Left headline: *"Moves Away"*
> Left body: *"Dependency tracking / Artefact versioning / Consistency enforcement / Documentation population"*
> Right headline: *"Stays With You"*
> Right body: *"Structural decisions / Persona confidence calls / Drift resolution / Stakeholder framing / Approval"*

---

## 24 — The New Discipline

The designer who understands this — who treats the early, invisible, difficult, unshowable work of getting the foundations right as the primary professional obligation, and treats everything that follows as the pipeline's delivery of that work — is operating with the discipline the new way requires.

The designer who doesn't will produce more artefacts, faster, at higher fidelity, than the old way allowed.

And they will be wrong earlier, at greater scale, with better documentation.

The new discipline is not about learning to use the tools. It is about understanding what the tools cannot do — and showing up, every time, for the work that remains irreducibly human: the contact, the judgment, the honesty about what you know and what you don't, the willingness to challenge the brief before accepting it.

That work has always been where design lived. It has never been harder to prioritise, or more consequential to neglect.

> **Slide 24 — Big statement**
> *"The designers who thrive in an agentic process will not be the ones who generate the most. They will be the ones who decide best."*

## 25 — Discussion

Three questions to take back:

**What decisions do you currently wish you had more time for?**
If the overhead disappeared tomorrow — the tracking, the consistency enforcement, the documentation — where would you put that time? The answer reveals what you actually believe design is for.

**Where does judgment live in your current process?**
Map the last project you shipped. Where were the moments that required a human who had done the research, absorbed the tension, and made a call? How much of the project's outcome depended on those moments going well?

**What overhead would you most want gone?**
Not what the AI could theoretically remove — what specifically, from the last six months, consumed time that was not design thinking? That is where the pipeline creates genuine space.
